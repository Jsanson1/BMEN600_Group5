## A. Participants and the two datasets

The analysis includes 155 gaitpdb participants (87 with PD, 68 controls) and 169 WearGait-PD participants (94 with PD, 75 controls); Section III-B gives the exclusions, and Table I describes both samples. They are built differently. In WearGait-PD the controls are older than the patients (median 75 against 68 years), the reverse of gaitpdb (64 against 68). Controls include more women than patients in both datasets (44% against 36% in gaitpdb, 55% against 35% in WearGait-PD). Among the analysed participants, two of WearGait-PD's three sites contributed one group each, 46 controls at Johns Hopkins Bayview and 66 patients at the Johns Hopkins Outpatient Center (whose three controls had no insole data), so only VA Puget Sound (29 controls, 28 patients) contributed both. Disease stage overlaps: Hoehn and Yahr stage 2 (range 2 to 3) in gaitpdb and modified stage 2 (range 1 to 4, missing for 5) in WearGait-PD. WearGait-PD's participants contributed far fewer clean strides, a median of 31 (PD) and 33 (controls) on the foot with fewer, against 82 and 80.

The datasets also differ in ways that involve no diagnosis (Fig. 4). Over all participants, WearGait-PD's stride-time and swing-time variability are about twice gaitpdb's (median coefficient of variation 4.96% against 2.41%, and 8.19% against 3.67%), its swing fraction is lower (33.7% against 37.3%) and its stride-time asymmetry, which is not a model input, is eleven times higher (0.558% against 0.0505%). Its straight walking comes in short bouts, a median of 2 consecutive clean strides against 18 in gaitpdb, so 62.1% of its clean strides open or close a bout (10.0% in gaitpdb). In bouts of five strides or more, the first stride lasts on average 6.7% longer than the participant's median stride for that foot and the last 4.2% longer, against 0.5% and 1.2% in gaitpdb (Fig. 4B). Without the first and last stride of every bout, WearGait-PD's median variability would be 2.83% for stride time and 5.80% for swing time, but 91 of its 169 participants would fall below the minimum of 10 clean strides on a foot. From the five model inputs alone, each model tells the two datasets apart with an AUC of 0.94 (95% CI 0.91 to 0.97).

## B. Separation from unseen participants to an unseen cohort

Fig. 5 shows how well the models, on the five gait timing features (Section III-D), separate PD from controls as the test moves further from the training data. Our plan named the combined analysis of both datasets its main analysis and built its research gap on the cross-cohort test; Fig. 5 shows both, after the evaluations within gaitpdb.

Within gaitpdb, the logistic regression reached an AUC of 0.79 (95% CI 0.70 to 0.88) on participants it had not seen and 0.73 (0.65 to 0.81) on the predictions for held-out sub-studies, each sub-study held out in turn and the three sets of predictions pooled (0.80 for Ga, 0.80 for Ju and 0.66 for Si). Computed from pooled predictions in both cases, the drop was 0.053 (0.022 to 0.088); pooling the predictions of separately fitted models can itself move an AUC (a chance model's pooled value across sub-studies is 0.47). The support vector machine reached 0.78 (0.68 to 0.87) and 0.72 (0.64 to 0.79), a drop of 0.056 (0.016 to 0.096), and the random forest 0.75 (0.65 to 0.85) and 0.70 (0.62 to 0.78), a drop of 0.046 (0.010 to 0.080). No model was significantly better than a one-feature logistic regression on swing-time asymmetry, fitted on the same splits, which reached 0.73 (0.64 to 0.81) and 0.71 (0.63 to 0.79): within protocol, the differences from it were +0.063 (−0.006 to +0.131, Holm-adjusted p = 0.80), +0.051 (−0.026 to +0.128, Holm-adjusted p = 1.00) and +0.025 (−0.055 to +0.104, Holm-adjusted p = 1.00); across protocols, +0.023 (−0.048 to +0.090), +0.010 (−0.063 to +0.083) and −0.009 (−0.090 to +0.073). Nor did the models differ significantly from each other: within protocol, the logistic regression minus the random forest was +0.038 (−0.006 to +0.082, Holm-adjusted p = 0.86), the logistic regression minus the support vector machine +0.012 (−0.027 to +0.051) and the support vector machine minus the random forest +0.026 (−0.020 to +0.072; Holm-adjusted p = 1.00 for both). Age and sex alone reached 0.60 (0.51 to 0.69) and 0.58 (0.49 to 0.67), and adding them to the gait features changed the logistic regression's AUC by +0.006 (−0.031 to +0.043). At a probability threshold of 0.5 the balanced accuracy of the logistic regression and the support vector machine was 0.68 (0.59 to 0.77). At 90% specificity the logistic regression's sensitivity was 0.53 (0.36 to 0.63) within protocol and 0.47 (0.34 to 0.57) across protocols, the support vector machine's 0.53 (0.37 to 0.63) and 0.43 (0.28 to 0.56), and the random forest's within protocol 0.43 (0.31 to 0.58).

With both datasets combined in the same participant-grouped cross-validation, stratified by diagnosis and by dataset, the AUC was 0.63 (0.56 to 0.69), 0.63 (0.56 to 0.70) and 0.67 (0.61 to 0.73). Among gaitpdb's test participants it was 0.71 (0.62 to 0.81), 0.71 (0.61 to 0.81) and 0.73 (0.64 to 0.81), and among WearGait-PD's 0.58 (0.48 to 0.69), 0.57 (0.48 to 0.65) and 0.62 (0.54 to 0.71). Adding age and sex raised the combined AUC to 0.68 (0.62 to 0.74), 0.74 (0.69 to 0.80) and 0.70 (0.64 to 0.76), mostly among WearGait-PD's participants, 0.74 (0.66 to 0.82), 0.75 (0.68 to 0.82) and 0.71 (0.63 to 0.79). Among gaitpdb's, the AUC fell to 0.63 (0.54 to 0.72) for the logistic regression and 0.71 (0.63 to 0.79) for the random forest and rose to 0.74 (0.66 to 0.82) for the support vector machine.

Fitted on all of gaitpdb and applied once to WearGait-PD, the models reached AUCs of 0.53 (0.45 to 0.62), 0.53 (0.45 to 0.62) and 0.57 (0.49 to 0.66): intervals that include 0.5, so the separation could not be distinguished from chance. At 90% specificity on WearGait-PD's own predictions, the sensitivity was 0.19, 0.19 and 0.10, against 0.53, 0.53 and 0.43 within gaitpdb. Carried over, the threshold that gave 90% specificity in gaitpdb flagged most of WearGait-PD's participants, patients and controls alike: sensitivity 0.93, 0.90 and 0.77, specificity 0.12, 0.12 and 0.28.

## C. Checks of the cross-cohort result

These checks were fixed, with the main test, before any model was applied to WearGait-PD's diagnoses (Table II). None brought the AUC near its value within gaitpdb. Standardising each dataset's inputs with its own mean and standard deviation gave 0.54 for all three models; leaving out the two variability features gave 0.52, 0.53 and 0.58; robust variability measures, 0.52, 0.52 and 0.54; adding age and sex, 0.51, 0.47 and 0.55; and a contact threshold fixed at 20 N instead of 5% of each recording's force level, which admits 164 WearGait-PD participants, 0.53, 0.53 and 0.54. At VA Puget Sound, the one site with both groups (57 participants), the AUC was 0.56 (0.40 to 0.70), 0.55 (0.40 to 0.70) and 0.55 (0.40 to 0.70). In 56 pairs of a patient and a control of the same sex within three years of age, it was 0.65 (0.54 to 0.75), 0.65 (0.54 to 0.75) and 0.67 (0.57 to 0.77), the only version with intervals above 0.5; its difference from the main test was not tested. Within gaitpdb, cutting the test participants' clean strides to WearGait-PD's counts lowered the AUC by 0.008, 0.009 and 0.001. At 20 N, the logistic regression's AUC within gaitpdb was numerically higher than at 5%: 0.83 (0.76 to 0.90) within protocol and 0.80 (0.73 to 0.87) across protocols, against 0.79 and 0.73 (the difference was not tested).

## D. Which features carry the separation in gaitpdb

Alone, on the same splits as the models, swing-time asymmetry reached a within-protocol AUC of 0.73 (0.64 to 0.81), swing fraction 0.71 (0.61 to 0.82), swing-time variability 0.69 (0.61 to 0.78), stride-time variability 0.66 (0.56 to 0.75) and mean stride time 0.56 (0.46 to 0.65). Swing fraction is lower in PD and the other four are higher (rank AUC over all 155 participants: 0.28 for swing fraction, that is 0.72 with the direction reversed, and 0.55 to 0.73 for the others). In the penalised logistic regression fitted on all 155 participants, the odds of PD were 2.31 times higher (95% bootstrap interval 1.74 to 3.40) per standard deviation of log(1 + swing-time asymmetry) and 1.66 times higher (1.06 to 2.60) per standard deviation of stride-time variability, and 0.45 times (0.30 to 0.67) per standard deviation of swing fraction; the intervals for swing-time variability (1.13, 0.74 to 1.96) and mean stride time (0.86, 0.58 to 1.29) include 1, and no input's variance inflation factor exceeded 3.4. In the random forest, shuffling swing-time asymmetry among the test participants lowered the test-fold AUC by 0.103 on average over the five folds of one cross-validation repeat (SD across folds 0.072), and shuffling swing fraction by 0.030 (SD 0.058); shuffling any of the other three changed it by 0.013 or less. In the support vector machine the same two inputs mattered most, 0.135 (SD 0.073) and 0.066 (SD 0.055), and the other three 0.014 or less.

## E. Exploratory checks after the cross-cohort test

These checks were written after the cross-cohort result was known. Inside WearGait-PD, each input alone had a rank AUC between 0.52 and 0.58 (no intervals computed), and a logistic regression trained and tested within WearGait-PD under the same cross-validation reached 0.59 (0.50 to 0.69). Swing fraction, lower in PD in gaitpdb (rank AUC 0.28), had a rank AUC of 0.53 in WearGait-PD and 0.43 at VA Puget Sound. Age alone had a rank AUC of 0.26 in WearGait-PD, where controls are older, and 0.61 in gaitpdb. Among WearGait-PD's controls, the cross-cohort models' predicted probability of PD rose with age (Spearman ρ 0.641, p = 5.7 × 10⁻¹⁰; 0.623, p = 2.4 × 10⁻⁹; and 0.556, p = 2.2 × 10⁻⁷; n = 75), and among its patients ρ was 0.210 (p = 0.042), 0.221 (p = 0.032) and 0.240 (p = 0.02; n = 94); the difference between the groups' correlations was not tested. Of the 56 age-matched pairs of Section C, 49 join a patient and a control from different sites.

Diamonds, at the first two levels only: a one-feature model on swing-time asymmetry.
