## A. Participants and the two datasets

The analyses include 155 gaitpdb participants (87 with PD, 68 controls) and 169 WearGait-PD participants (94 with PD, 75 controls) (Table I). The two cohorts are built differently. In WearGait-PD the controls are older than the patients (median 75 against 68 years), the reverse of gaitpdb (64 against 68), and more of them are women (55% against 35%; 44% and 36% in gaitpdb). Two of WearGait-PD's three sites contributed only one group, controls at Johns Hopkins Bayview and patients at the Johns Hopkins Outpatient Center, so only VA Puget Sound (29 controls, 28 PD) recruited both. The patients' disease stage overlaps: Hoehn and Yahr stage 2 (range 2 to 3) in gaitpdb and modified stage 2 (range 1 to 4, missing for 5) in WearGait-PD. Each WearGait-PD participant contributed far fewer clean strides, a median of 31 to 33 on the foot with fewer, against 80 to 82.

The datasets differ before any diagnosis label is used (Fig. 4). Across all participants, WearGait-PD's stride-time and swing-time variability are about twice gaitpdb's (median coefficient of variation 4.96% against 2.41%, and 8.19% against 3.67%), its swing fraction is lower (33.7% against 37.3%) and its stride-time asymmetry eleven times higher (0.558% against 0.0505%). Its straight walking comes in short bouts, a median of 2 consecutive clean strides against 18 in gaitpdb, so 62.1% of its clean strides open or close a bout (10.0% in gaitpdb). In bouts of five or more strides, the first stride lasts on average 6.7% longer than the participant's median stride and the last 4.2% longer, against 0.5% and 1.2% in gaitpdb (Fig. 4B). Dropping one stride at each end of every bout lowers WearGait-PD's median variability to 2.83% for stride time and 5.80% for swing time, but leaves a median of 9 strides on the weaker foot, below the minimum of 10 for 91 of its 169 participants. From the five model inputs alone, a model tells the two datasets apart with an AUC of 0.940 (95% CI 0.909 to 0.971).

## B. Separation from unseen participants to an unseen cohort

Fig. 5 sets the main results side by side. Within gaitpdb, the logistic regression on the five gait features separated PD from controls with an AUC of 0.79 (95% CI 0.70 to 0.88) in participants it had not seen, and 0.73 (0.65 to 0.81) when a whole sub-study was held out, a drop of 0.05 (0.02 to 0.09); by held-out sub-study the AUC was 0.80 for Ga, 0.80 for Ju and 0.66 for Si. The random forest reached 0.75 (0.65 to 0.85) and 0.70 (0.62 to 0.78). Neither model was significantly better than swing-time asymmetry alone, which reached 0.73 (0.64 to 0.81) and 0.71 (0.63 to 0.79): the logistic regression's advantage was +0.063 AUC (−0.006 to +0.131, Holm-adjusted p = 0.43) within protocol and +0.023 (−0.048 to +0.090) across protocols. The two models did not differ significantly from each other (+0.038, −0.006 to +0.082). Age and sex alone reached 0.60 (0.51 to 0.69) and 0.58 (0.49 to 0.67), and adding them to the gait features changed the logistic regression's AUC by +0.006 (−0.031 to +0.043). At a probability threshold of 0.5 the logistic regression's balanced accuracy was 0.68 (0.59 to 0.77); at 90% specificity its sensitivity was 0.53 (0.36 to 0.63) within protocol and 0.47 (0.34 to 0.57) across protocols.

With both datasets combined under the same participant-grouped cross-validation, the main analysis of our plan, the AUC was 0.628 (0.561 to 0.694) for the logistic regression and 0.666 (0.605 to 0.726) for the random forest. Within those predictions, gaitpdb's participants were ranked at 0.714 and 0.726 and WearGait-PD's at 0.583 (0.480 to 0.686) and 0.622 (0.535 to 0.708). Adding age and sex raised the combined AUC to 0.680 and 0.701, but only through WearGait-PD's participants (0.737 and 0.711), whose controls are the older group; gaitpdb's participants were ranked lower than without them (0.631 and 0.706). Age alone has an AUC of 0.261 in WearGait-PD and 0.607 in gaitpdb.

Fitted on all of gaitpdb and applied once to WearGait-PD, the models did not separate the groups: the AUC was 0.532 (0.449 to 0.621) for the logistic regression and 0.573 (0.486 to 0.659) for the random forest, intervals that include 0.5. Carried over, the threshold that gave 90% specificity in gaitpdb flagged most of WearGait-PD's participants, patients and controls alike: sensitivity 0.93 and specificity 0.12 for the logistic regression, 0.77 and 0.28 for the random forest.

## C. Checks of the cross-cohort result

No version of the cross-cohort test recovered the separation (Table II). Standardising each dataset's inputs with its own mean and standard deviation left the AUC at 0.535 and 0.538; leaving out the two variability features gave 0.521 and 0.576, the robust variability measures 0.519 and 0.539, and adding age and sex 0.509 and 0.554. At a contact threshold fixed at 20 N, which admits 164 WearGait-PD participants, the AUC was 0.534 and 0.540. At VA Puget Sound, the one site with both groups (57 participants), it was 0.555 (0.398 to 0.701) and 0.554 (0.403 to 0.698). In 56 pairs of a patient and a control of the same sex and within three years of age it was higher, 0.646 (0.539 to 0.750) and 0.670 (0.566 to 0.771), with intervals that overlap those of the main test. The smaller number of strides does not explain the failure: within gaitpdb, recomputing the test participants' features from WearGait-PD's stride counts changed the AUC from 0.789 to 0.780 for the logistic regression and from 0.751 to 0.750 for the random forest. The 20 N threshold did change the result within gaitpdb, where the logistic regression reached 0.831 (0.762 to 0.899) within protocol and 0.803 (0.732 to 0.869) across protocols, against 0.789 and 0.734 at the main threshold; this difference was not tested.

## D. Which features carry the separation in gaitpdb

Taken alone, swing-time asymmetry separated the groups best (AUC 0.728, higher in PD), followed by swing fraction (0.283, lower in PD), swing-time variability (0.692), stride-time variability (0.657) and mean stride time (0.553). In the logistic regression, the odds of PD rose by 2.31 times (95% bootstrap interval 1.74 to 3.40) per standard deviation of swing-time asymmetry and by 1.66 times (1.06 to 2.60) per standard deviation of stride-time variability, and fell to 0.45 (0.30 to 0.67) per standard deviation of swing fraction; the intervals for swing-time variability (1.13, 0.74 to 1.96) and mean stride time (0.86, 0.58 to 1.29) include 1, and no input's variance inflation factor exceeded 3.4. In the random forest, permuting swing-time asymmetry lowered the AUC by 0.103 (SD 0.072) and swing fraction by 0.030 (SD 0.058); permuting any of the other three changed it by 0.013 or less.

## E. Exploratory checks after the cross-cohort test

These checks were written after the cross-cohort result was known and are exploratory. Inside WearGait-PD the inputs barely separate the groups: each alone gives an AUC between 0.520 and 0.584, and a logistic regression trained and tested within WearGait-PD under the same cross-validation reaches 0.595 (0.500 to 0.690). Swing fraction, lower in PD in gaitpdb (AUC 0.283), is not lower in PD in WearGait-PD (0.527). Within WearGait-PD's controls, the predicted probability of PD from the cross-cohort models rises with age (Spearman ρ 0.641 for the logistic regression and 0.556 for the random forest), more than within its patients (0.210 and 0.240). The age-matched analysis of Section C cannot separate age from site, because 49 of its 56 pairs join a patient and a control from different sites.
