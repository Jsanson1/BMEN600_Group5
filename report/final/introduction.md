## I. Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disease, affecting more than 6 million people worldwide after a 2.5-fold rise in prevalence over a generation [S26]. It affects at least 1% of people over 60 [S15], and the number living with it is projected to reach 25.2 million by 2050, mainly because populations are ageing [S2]. Its diagnosis is still clinical, based on the history and on the cardinal motor signs of bradykinesia, rest tremor and rigidity, with changes in posture and gait [S15], [S26]. Checked against pathology, that diagnosis was correct in 80.6% of cases across eleven studies, in 73.8% when made by non-experts, and least often early in the disease, with no improvement over 25 years [S1]. By the time PD is diagnosed, more than half of the dopaminergic neurons have been lost [S15], and no approved biomarker yet identifies patients before that damage [S27]. Genetic and imaging tests already support the clinical work-up and fluid and tissue markers are under investigation, but none yet allows a definitive diagnosis from the earliest stages [S26].

These limits fall hardest on older adults with early or uncertain signs who are seen far from specialist care. In Canada, the number of practising neurologists ranges from 3 to 13 per 100,000 people across the provinces; only Ontario and British Columbia reach the average of high-income countries, and access remains concentrated in large urban centres, leaving rural and remote communities underserved [S29]. People with PD in a rural US state named the lack of local specialists and the travel needed to see them among their barriers to care, and themselves proposed telehealth as part of the remedy [S30]. An objective measurement that a primary-care or community clinic could record, and that a neurologist could review remotely, would help decide who should be seen by a specialist first. Such a tool would be a screening aid that flags people for referral, not a diagnostic test. Because most people such a clinic sees do not have PD, it would need to work at a high specificity, and its value would depend on how reliably it ranks people with PD above people without it in the clinic where it is used, not only in the one where it was developed.

Gait is a natural candidate for that measurement. Gait impairment is among the most common and disabling features of PD, yet it is rarely quantified in routine care, and measuring speed, variability and asymmetry, especially under challenging conditions such as dual tasking or turning, makes its assessment more sensitive [S3]. The changes are well described at the group level. In the studies whose recordings form the PhysioNet "Gait in Parkinson's Disease" database (gaitpdb), people with PD walked with shorter strides and greater stride-to-stride variability than controls [S6]; their variability increased under a secondary task [S4], their swing-time variability did not depend on walking speed [S5], and rhythmic auditory cueing modulated their gait [S6]. Such abnormalities have traditionally been judged by neurologists in partly subjective evaluations during short and infrequent appointments, and wearable sensors with computational analysis are now seen as a route to more objective assessment, although their standardisation for clinical use is still missing [S28]. Force-sensing insoles are among the simplest of these sensors: the moments each foot strikes and leaves the ground give stride, stance and swing times, their variability and their left-right asymmetry, without cameras or a gait laboratory.

Most machine-learning work on this problem has used a single dataset. A 2026 survey of plantar-pressure studies found gaitpdb in 82 of 102 studies, a de facto standard, with support vector machines and random forests the most common conventional classifiers and k-fold cross-validation the most common evaluation [S32]; an earlier review of wearable-sensor studies also found gait the most common input and support vector machines the most common algorithm [S10]. Reported performance on gaitpdb is high, up to 98% accuracy for detecting and staging PD together [S11], but how the data are split matters. Participants in this database contributed one to seven recordings each, and when recordings rather than participants are divided between training and test folds, the same person can appear in both. Saeb et al. showed that such "record-wise" validation "often massively overestimates" accuracy, and that almost half of the wearable-sensor studies they reviewed had used it [S12]; recent work on these data still describes k-fold cross-validation over recordings without saying whether each participant's recordings were kept together [S11]. Studies that split by participant report less. Deep networks on windows of the force signal reached a mean area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.94 under repeated subject-wise splits [S13], and a participant-level pipeline reached 0.99 only with Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) and Hoehn and Yahr scores among its inputs, 0.84 with gait features alone, and between 0.50 and 1.00 when each of the database's three sub-studies was held out in turn [S14]. Both studies found variability features informative [S13], [S14].

These participant-level studies are the closest to ours, and they leave one question open. Holding out a sub-study changes the walking protocol and the participants, but not the insole system, which was the same in all three [S7]. Both studies evaluated their models only within this database, and the second calls for external validation [S13], [S14]. The 2026 survey likewise judges testing on an entirely independent dataset to be the more rigorous evaluation, critical for assessing generalisability, and notes that current algorithms are not robust on external cohorts [S32]. Experience elsewhere in medical machine learning shows why this matters. Networks trained to detect pneumonia on chest radiographs did worse on another hospital system's images than on new images from their own in three of five comparisons; models trained on pooled data from hospitals with different disease prevalence did better on new pooled data but not on external data; and the networks could identify which hospital system a radiograph came from [S31]. A gait classifier could likewise learn the marks of a laboratory, a device or a recruited population rather than of the disease. WearGait-PD, published in 2026 by a different group, with different insoles, a different walking protocol and a different population (100 people with PD and 85 controls at three sites in the United States) [S9], [S20], now makes it possible to test this directly for timing features from foot force. Its recordings are also closer to what a clinic could collect: four passes along a 16-foot walkway, turning beyond each end [S9], against about two minutes of walking in each gaitpdb recording [S7]. That matters for the variability features, because in confined spaces about 15 to 20 strides are needed for a reliable estimate of variability, and strides after a turn are longer in time than the rest [S21].

The open question is therefore not whether gait differs in PD, but whether a small set of interpretable timing features computed from foot force alone, without clinical scores, separates people with PD from controls aged 50 and over when tested the way a screening aid would be used: on people, recording protocols and cohorts the model has never seen. To answer it, we derived five timing features (mean stride time, stride-time and swing-time variability, the swing fraction of the stride, and swing-time asymmetry) from the per-foot force of both datasets with one shared pipeline. We compared a logistic regression, a support vector machine and a random forest under the same participant-grouped evaluation at four levels: unseen participants and unseen sub-studies within gaitpdb, the two datasets combined, and models trained on gaitpdb and applied once to WearGait-PD. Before any model was applied to WearGait-PD's diagnoses, we fixed a set of checks that separate a shift in the features' level from a loss of their ranking and that test the roles of stride count, contact threshold, recording site and age. Our contribution is this participant-level, cross-cohort evaluation of simple gait-timing models, with every step in public code. Its answer is split: the features separated the groups moderately within gaitpdb, on unseen participants (AUC 0.75 to 0.79) and largely across its protocols (0.70 to 0.73), but not in the independent cohort (0.53 to 0.57), whichever model was used.
