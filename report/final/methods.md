## A. Datasets

The development dataset is the PhysioNet "Gait in Parkinson's Disease" database, version 1.0.0 (gaitpdb) [S7], [S8], accessed on 5 October 2026; its 311 files were checked against the published SHA-256 checksums. It holds 306 recordings of 93 people with PD and 73 controls from three sub-studies: Ga, a dual-task study [S4]; Ju, a study of rhythmic auditory cueing [S6]; and Si, a study of gait speed and treadmill walking [S5], [S19]. Participants walked at their usual pace along a corridor, for about 2 min in Ga and Si and for 100 m (four lengths of a 25 m corridor) in Ju [S6], [S7]. Eight force sensors under each foot recorded vertical force at 100 Hz, and the files give each foot's total force [S7]. A demographic table gives age, sex, height, weight, Hoehn and Yahr stage, UPDRS score and walking speed. We use each participant's usual walk (walk 01) only. In Si, the usual walk is assumed to be unassisted overground walking, because the database does not state its condition and its walking speeds match the source study's unassisted condition [S19].

The external dataset is WearGait-PD [S9], [S20], collected by the US Food and Drug Administration with Johns Hopkins Medicine (two sites) and the VA Puget Sound Health Care System, all with the same hardware and protocol [S9]. We use its initial cross-sectional release, 185 participants (100 with PD, 85 controls) [S9]: the two participant sheets, downloaded on 14 October 2026, and the files of three self-paced straight-walking tasks for every participant, downloaded on 2 November 2026 (553 files, each checked against its Synapse checksum). In SelfPace, participants make four passes across a 16 ft (4.9 m) instrumented walkway, starting about 5 ft before it and turning beyond it; in SelfPace_mat they start, stop and turn on the walkway; SelfPace_matTURN has five passes, alternating the direction of the turns [S9]. Each file holds, at 100 Hz, the total force under each foot from pressure insoles with 16 sensors each, the walkway's foot contacts and video labels of each moment of the task ("Walk", "Turn" and others) [S9].

In both datasets, one independent case is one participant, labelled PD or control as each dataset records. A gaitpdb participant contributes one recording and a WearGait-PD participant three, whose strides are pooled; a participant's strides are never split between training and test.

## B. Participants

Following the intended use, screening people aged 50 or over, every analysis excludes younger participants (3 in gaitpdb, 4 in WearGait-PD). The features need enough clean strides (Section D): at least 40 on each foot in gaitpdb and 10 in WearGait-PD, whose walks are much shorter. Of gaitpdb's 162 remaining participants with a recording, one has no recorded walking speed, which locating its turns requires, and six have too few clean strides, leaving 155 (87 with PD, 68 controls). Of WearGait-PD's 181, 11 have no insole data and one has too few clean strides, leaving 169 (94 with PD, 75 controls). Table I (Section IV) describes them.

## C. Responsible use

gaitpdb is openly available under the Open Data Commons Attribution License 1.0 and is cited as its terms require [S7], [S8]. WearGait-PD is released under CC BY 4.0, and access requires a Synapse account in a person's name with acceptance of Synapse's terms of use [S9], [S20]. Both datasets identify participants only by study codes. Our repository contains no recordings; it holds derived tables with one row per participant (the dataset's own code, age, sex and the timing features) and summaries. [For the group: confirm that the Synapse terms allow sharing these derived per-participant tables; CC BY 4.0 itself allows sharing with attribution.] Neither sample represents a screening population: both compare diagnosed patients with volunteer controls; men make up about two thirds of the patients in both; gaitpdb records no ethnicity or socioeconomic information [S7], and WearGait-PD records race but nothing socioeconomic [S9]; WearGait-PD's controls are older than its patients, the reverse of gaitpdb, and include people with mild movement disorders such as essential tremor [S9]; and two of WearGait-PD's three sites contributed participants of only one group to the analysis. Medication state was not controlled in WearGait-PD [S9] and is not recorded in gaitpdb [S7]. The clinical diagnosis that labels both datasets is itself imperfect [S1].

## D. From force signals to features

A foot is in contact while its total force exceeds 5% of the recording's force level, defined as the mean over the two feet of each foot's 95th percentile of total force; contact and swing phases shorter than 0.1 s are merged into their neighbours. Heel strike and toe off are the upward and downward crossings, from which each stride's stride, stance and swing times follow. This threshold was chosen, before any classification result, as the rule whose insole contacts agreed best with WearGait-PD's walkway on 19,200 straight-walking footfalls of 170 participants (83.4% matched one to one), and the same rule is applied to both datasets; the midterm plan's fixed 20 N is kept as a sensitivity analysis.

Turns and their neighbouring strides are removed differently in the two datasets, because only WearGait-PD labels them. In gaitpdb, turns are expected every corridor length divided by the participant's walking speed (25 m for Ga and Ju [S4], [S6], 35 m for Si [S19]); the walk's own turn rhythm, the peak of a Lomb-Scargle periodogram of the stride series near that interval, sets the spacing, and each predicted turn is confirmed by a stride at least 10% longer than that foot's median while the other foot is also disturbed. Every stride overlapping the 2 s either side of a turn centre is removed, plus one stride either side, as is any stride that looks like two strides merged by a missed heel strike (1.75 to 2.25 times the foot's median while the other foot is undisturbed). In WearGait-PD, a stride is kept only if every sample is labelled "Walk"; gaps of up to 50 ms in the insole signal are filled by linear interpolation and strides touching longer gaps are removed. In both datasets a stride must last 0.5 to 2.5 s with swing between 10% and 70% of it, and the first and last stride of each recording are dropped.

Five features are computed per participant from the clean strides of both feet: mean stride time; stride-time and swing-time variability, each the coefficient of variation (100 × SD / mean) computed per foot and averaged over the feet; swing fraction, the mean swing time as a share of its stride; and swing-time asymmetry, 100 × |left − right| / mean of the two feet's mean swing times. The midterm plan's sixth feature, stride-time asymmetry, was dropped before any WearGait-PD label was used: in a continuous walk it is near zero by construction (median 0.05% in gaitpdb), it did not separate the groups in gaitpdb (AUC 0.52), and it mostly reflects how many strides a participant has.

## E. Models

The main models are fitted on the five gait features. A logistic regression with an L2 penalty standardises its inputs, enters swing-time asymmetry as log(1 + x), and chooses its penalty strength among 17 values from 10⁻⁴ to 10⁴ by an inner five-fold cross-validation on log-loss within each training set. A random forest [S22] of 300 trees chooses its maximum depth (2, 3, 5 or unlimited) and the number of inputs tried at each split (1, 2 or 5) by the log-loss of its out-of-bag predictions within each training set. [A support vector machine is added under the same evaluation when task T-020 lands.] Two further input sets serve as comparisons: the gait features with age and sex, and age and sex alone.

## F. Evaluation

The primary measure is the area under the ROC curve (AUC), which scores how well a model ranks people with PD above controls without fixing a threshold or a prevalence, neither of which is settled for screening. The research question asks whether the separation holds for participants, recording protocols and cohorts a model has not seen, so the models are evaluated at four levels (Fig. 2). Within gaitpdb, five-fold cross-validation stratified by diagnosis and grouped by participant is repeated 20 times; the AUC is the mean over the 100 test folds, with a 95% interval corrected for the overlap between training sets [S17]. Across gaitpdb's protocols, each sub-study is held out in turn and the AUC is computed on the three held-out sets of predictions pooled, with a 95% participant bootstrap interval (2,000 resamples). In the combined analysis, which our plan names its main analysis, the same repeated cross-validation runs over all 324 participants, stratified by diagnosis and by dataset, and the AUC is also computed within each dataset's test participants. In the cross-cohort test, on which the research gap rests, each model is fitted once on all of gaitpdb, with all tuning inside gaitpdb, and applied once to WearGait-PD, with a 95% participant bootstrap interval; the threshold that gives 90% specificity on gaitpdb's out-of-fold predictions is carried over to show what a fixed operating point would do.

The cross-cohort analysis and its checks were committed before any model was applied to WearGait-PD's diagnoses. The checks are: inputs standardised within each dataset, which removes differences in level and spread and so separates a shift from a loss of ranking; the models without the two variability features, and with robust variability (scaled median absolute deviation) in place of the coefficients of variation; age and sex added; the whole pipeline at a fixed 20 N; VA Puget Sound alone, the one site with both groups; and pairs of a patient and a control of the same sex within three years of age, matched greedily from the youngest patient. Two further checks need no diagnosis labels from WearGait-PD: whether the five features tell the two datasets apart (a model predicting the dataset of origin), and how much stride count alone costs, by recomputing each gaitpdb test participant's features from only as many clean strides per foot as a randomly drawn WearGait-PD participant has, taken as 13 runs of consecutive strides from evenly spaced stretches of the walk (20 draws).

Results are compared with chance, with age and sex alone, and with each feature alone (a logistic regression on that feature, fitted on the same splits). Paired differences in AUC between models within gaitpdb use the corrected repeated cross-validation t-test [S18] with Holm's adjustment [S23]. Sensitivity at 90% specificity and the predictive values at a prevalence of 1% describe what an operating point would mean for screening [S15]. Checks written after the cross-cohort result was known, such as models trained within WearGait-PD and the relation of the predictions to age, are reported separately as exploratory.

## G. Software and reproducibility

The analysis is written in Python 3.13 with NumPy 2.5.3, pandas 3.0.5, SciPy 1.18.1, scikit-learn 1.9.1 [S24] and Matplotlib 3.11.2; the repository's README lists the steps to obtain the data and run every script. A shared package (`src/gaitpdb/`) holds the event detection, cleaning, features, evaluation and cross-dataset analyses, so every model uses the same participants, splits and metrics; one script per output writes each table and figure, and `scripts/run_all.py` runs them all in order. The primary results come from `scripts/run_cross_cohort.py` and `scripts/compare_models.py`, Fig. 5 from `scripts/make_figure5.py`. A rerun of the whole analysis from a clean copy of the repository reproduced every output it writes exactly, apart from a line recording the commit [to repeat at the submitted commit], and 125 unit tests on synthetic signals check the shared code. An AI assistant (Claude, Anthropic) wrote much of the code at the team's direction; the data were processed only by that code, which is in the repository, and no AI tool labelled, transformed or analysed the data directly (see AI-Assisted Work).
