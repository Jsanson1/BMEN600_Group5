## A. Answer to the research question

Within one cohort, a handful of timing features computed from foot force alone separate people with PD from controls aged 50 and over moderately, and the separation largely survives a change of recording protocol: in gaitpdb, the logistic regression reached an AUC of 0.79 (95% CI 0.70 to 0.88) on unseen participants and 0.73 (0.65 to 0.81) on unseen sub-studies, and the random forest 0.75 and 0.70. Swing-time asymmetry carries much of this: alone it reached 0.73 and 0.71, and neither model was significantly better, though gains of up to 0.13 cannot be excluded. The separation did not survive a change of cohort. Fitted on gaitpdb and applied once to WearGait-PD, the models reached 0.53 (0.45 to 0.62) and 0.57 (0.49 to 0.66), not distinguishable from chance, and combining the two datasets, the main analysis of our plan, did not recover it (0.63 and 0.67 overall, 0.58 and 0.62 among WearGait-PD's participants). Our answer is therefore split: yes within a cohort, mainly through swing-time asymmetry and swing fraction; no across these two cohorts.

## B. Relation to previous work

Our within-cohort AUC of 0.79 sits well below the 98% accuracy reported on this database for detecting and staging PD together, from cross-validation that does not say whether each participant's recordings were kept in one fold [S11]. Splitting a participant's recordings between training and test lets a model recognise the person rather than the disease, and record-wise validation "often massively overestimates" accuracy [S12]; our splits never do so. Studies that split by participant report less than [S11] but more than we do: a mean AUC of 0.94 for deep networks on windows of the force signal, though whether per window or per participant is not stated [S13], and 0.84 for gradient boosting on gait features [S14], which our logistic regression approached at the 20 N threshold (0.83). Jin's results depended on which sub-study was held out (0.50 to 1.00 [S14]); ours varied less (0.80, 0.80 and 0.66). The database's source studies reported higher stride-time variability in PD [S5], [S6] and higher swing-time variability [S5]; alone, our coefficients of variation separated the groups less well than swing-time asymmetry (rank AUC 0.66 and 0.69 against 0.73), although a robust measure of stride-time variability matched it (0.73). Two of the closest studies name validation on an external cohort as the next step [S11], [S14]. Our cross-cohort test is such a validation for simpler models, and they fail it: participant-level validation within one database is necessary, but it does not show that a model will work in another cohort.

## C. Why the models do not transfer

The two datasets differ in the five features themselves, enough for a model to tell them apart (AUC 0.94). We weighed five explanations against the evidence.

*Walking protocol.* WearGait-PD's straight walking comes in short passes across a 4.9 m walkway [S9], bouts of a median of 2 clean strides against 18 in gaitpdb. Its "Walk" label ends where the next turn begins [S20], so a bout's last stride includes the slowing into the turn, and strides after a turn are longer than the rest of a walk, in patients with movement disorders more than in healthy older adults [S21]. Dropping one stride at each bout end would lower WearGait-PD's median variability from 4.96% to 2.83% for stride time and from 8.19% to 5.80% for swing time, against gaitpdb's 2.41% and 3.67%, so bout ends appear to account for most of the excess in stride-time variability but only about half of that in swing-time variability. The trimmed series, a median of 9 strides on the foot with fewer, are too short to settle this [S21], and the models without the variability features transferred no better (0.52 and 0.58).

*Number of strides.* WearGait-PD's participants contributed fewer clean strides (median 32 on the foot with fewer, against 81), but cutting gaitpdb's test participants to those counts cost at most 0.008 AUC.

*Age and the make-up of the groups.* WearGait-PD's controls were older than its patients (median 75 against 68 years), the reverse of gaitpdb (64 against 68), and included people with essential tremor [S9]. In the combined analysis, age and sex raised the ranking of WearGait-PD's participants and lowered gaitpdb's, so a model given age partly learns how each cohort was recruited. Exploratory checks suggest that the gait models also learned some of the gait of ageing in gaitpdb, where patients are older: among WearGait-PD's controls, their predicted probability of PD rose with age (Spearman ρ 0.641 and 0.556). Matching on sex and age raised the cross-cohort AUC to 0.65 and 0.67, but 49 of the 56 pairs join participants from different sites, and at VA Puget Sound, where the groups' ages were similar, it did not rise; how much age explains remains open. Disease stage overlaps (stage 2 to 3 in gaitpdb, modified stage 1 to 4 in WearGait-PD; Table I), and medication state could not be compared [S7], [S9].

*Hardware and event detection.* gaitpdb used eight force sensors under each foot [S7], WearGait-PD pressure insoles [S9], with one contact rule for both, chosen on agreement with WearGait-PD's walkway. The insoles agreed with the walkway more closely on stride time (95% limits ±0.09 s) than on stance and swing time (up to 0.15 s), so the phase-based features, swing fraction and swing-time asymmetry among them, carry more measurement error. At 20 N the cross-cohort result was the same (0.53 and 0.54): the threshold does not explain the result, though the hardware may contribute.

*Recruitment site.* Two of WearGait-PD's three sites contributed one group each. A model fitted on gaitpdb cannot have learned these sites, and at VA Puget Sound, the one site where neither site nor age is confounded with diagnosis, the AUC was 0.56 and 0.55: no sign of a site effect, but too few participants (57) to rule one out.

The low AUCs reflect ranking, not a shift: standardising each dataset's features left them at 0.54, and a pure shift would have lowered the specificity of a carried-over threshold, as happened (0.12), without lowering the AUC. Inside WearGait-PD, too, the features barely separated the groups (exploratory: a logistic regression trained and tested there reached 0.59, 0.50 to 0.69).

## D. What the study shows and what it does not

The study shows that models fitted on five foot-force timing features in gaitpdb did not transfer to WearGait-PD, in an analysis whose code and inputs were committed before any model was scored against WearGait-PD's diagnoses. The result holds at either contact threshold and is not explained by stride count, by shifts in the features' level and spread, or by the variability features. The inputs were chosen with WearGait-PD's unlabelled feature distributions in view, which if anything should have favoured transfer. The study suggests, but cannot establish, which differences matter most: the short passes inflate the variability features, yet models without them failed too; the scores' dependence on age points to ageing, yet the age-matched evidence is mixed. Protocol, hardware, population and site all change together between the two datasets, and two datasets cannot separate them. Nor does the study show that foot-force timing cannot detect PD elsewhere: the cross-cohort intervals reach 0.62 and 0.66, so a modest transfer is not excluded. The combined analysis, our plan's main analysis, is the hardest to read: it mixes two feature scales that a model can tell apart (AUC 0.94), so its AUC (0.63 and 0.67) describes neither one clinic nor a mixed screening population. Its interpretable parts are the AUCs within each dataset's test participants (gaitpdb 0.71 and 0.73, WearGait-PD 0.58 and 0.62) and, beside it, the cross-cohort test.

## E. What this means for patients and clinicians

Even within gaitpdb, the logistic regression found about half of the patients (sensitivity 0.53) at 90% specificity. PD affects at least 1% of people over 60 [S15]; at that prevalence this operating point gives a positive predictive value of 0.057, so about 94 of every 100 people flagged would not have PD. That is a best case: the patients were already diagnosed and the threshold was read off the same predictions it was evaluated on; for the early, undiagnosed disease a screening tool would be for, the sensitivity is unknown. Carried to WearGait-PD, gaitpdb's threshold flagged most participants, patients and controls alike (positive predictive value 0.011). Because the AUC summarises every possible threshold, recalibrating the threshold locally could not rescue the model, and the exploratory within-WearGait-PD result suggests that refitting it there would not be enough either. A classifier of this kind cannot be moved to another clinic or walking protocol without being validated there; each false positive costs the person flagged worry, further tests and a referral. Clinics that want such a tool should also record gait the same way, with the same device and walking protocol (we already applied one contact rule to both datasets); because our datasets differ in device, protocol and population at once, this study cannot show that standardising acquisition would be enough, only that without it the model did not carry over.

## F. Limitations

Six further limitations bound these conclusions. First, the samples are modest (155 and 169 participants) and the intervals correspondingly wide; with about fifteen cross-cohort comparisons per model, any single one, such as the age-matched rise, should be read with that number in mind. Second, both cohorts compare diagnosed patients with volunteer controls, not the early, undiagnosed disease and other gait disorders of a screening population, and the labels are clinical diagnoses, which in a meta-analysis agreed with pathology in 80.6% of cases [S1]. Third, gaitpdb's turns were located from walking-path timing rather than observed, and its Si usual walk was assumed to be overground (Section III), while WearGait-PD's turns were labelled from video, so its cleaning kept the stride next to each turn. Fourth, WearGait-PD's three tasks were pooled, which adds about 10% to stride-time variability, whereas each gaitpdb participant contributed one walk. Fifth, 11 of WearGait-PD's 181 participants aged 50 or over, 8 of them controls, had no insole data, and the contact threshold was chosen against its walkway, itself a measurement rather than ground truth. Sixth, gaitpdb records no ethnicity or socioeconomic information [S7], and WearGait-PD records race but nothing socioeconomic [S9], so how well either cohort represents a population that would be screened can be judged only in part.

## G. Next step

The most useful next step is to separate the walking protocol from the population. WearGait-PD's FreeWalk task, recorded with the same insoles on the same participants, leaves the session room for the hallways [S9]; comparing the features' separation in its straight corridor segments (stairs and chair cut out) with that in the short passes would show how much the passes remove, with population, hardware and site held fixed. Its sheets also hold the controls' motor examination scores, which would test directly whether controls with mild movement abnormalities look PD-like to the models. And because its protocol is rich in turns, one of the challenging conditions under which quantitative gait measures detect PD more sensitively [S3], turn-based measures could be taken from the same insole signals and evaluated within WearGait-PD under the same participant-grouped cross-validation.
