## A. Answer to the research question

Within one cohort, a handful of timing features computed from foot force alone separate people with PD from controls aged 50 and over moderately, and the separation largely survives a change of recording protocol. In gaitpdb, the logistic regression reached an AUC of 0.79 (95% CI 0.70 to 0.88) on unseen participants and 0.73 (0.65 to 0.81) on an unseen sub-study, a small loss (0.05, 0.02 to 0.09); the random forest reached 0.75 and 0.70. Swing-time asymmetry carries much of the separation: alone it reached 0.73 and 0.71, and neither model was significantly better, though gains of up to 0.13 cannot be excluded; swing fraction and, in the logistic regression, stride time variability also contributed. The separation did not survive a change of cohort. Fitted on gaitpdb and applied once to WearGait-PD, the logistic regression reached 0.532 (0.449 to 0.621) and the random forest 0.573 (0.486 to 0.659). Combining the two datasets, the main analysis of our plan, did not recover it: 0.628 and 0.666 overall, and 0.583 and 0.622 for WearGait-PD's participants. Our answer is therefore split: yes within a cohort, mainly through swing-time asymmetry and swing fraction; no across these two cohorts.

## B. Relation to previous work

Our within-cohort AUC of 0.79 (balanced accuracy 0.68) sits well below the 98% accuracy reported on this database for detecting and staging PD together, from cross-validation that does not say whether each participant's recordings were kept in one fold [S11]. Splitting a participant's recordings between training and test lets a model recognise the person rather than the disease, and record-wise validation "often massively overestimates" accuracy [S12]; our splits never do so. Studies that split by participant report less than [S11] but more than we do: a mean AUC of 0.94 for deep networks on windows of the force signal, though whether per window or per participant is not stated [S13], and 0.84 for gradient boosting on gait features [S14], which our logistic regression approached at the 20 N threshold (0.831). Jin's results depended on which sub-study was held out (0.50 to 1.00 [S14]); ours varied less (0.80, 0.80 and 0.66). The database's source studies reported higher stride time variability in PD [S5], [S6] and higher swing time variability [S5]; used alone, our coefficients of variation separated the groups less well than swing-time asymmetry (AUC 0.657 and 0.692 against 0.728), although a robust measure of stride time variability matched it (0.730). Two of the closest studies name validation on an external cohort as the next step [S11], [S14]. Our cross-cohort test is such a validation for simpler models, and they fail it: participant-level validation within one database is necessary, but it does not show that a model will work in another cohort.

## C. Why the models do not transfer

The two datasets differ before any model is fitted (Fig. 4): from the five features alone, a model told them apart with an AUC of 0.940. We weighed five explanations against the evidence.

*Walking protocol.* WearGait-PD's straight walking is short. In SelfPace, participants make four passes of about 8 m across a 16 ft (4.9 m) walkway, and in the other two tasks they start, stop and turn on the walkway itself [S9]. Its straight bouts had a median of 2 consecutive clean strides (18 in gaitpdb), so 62% of its clean strides open or close a bout, and its stride and swing time variability were about twice gaitpdb's. In one study, strides after a 180° turn lasted 3.2% longer than the walk average in patients with movement disorders and 1.4% longer in healthy older adults [S21]. Removing one stride at each bout end lowered WearGait-PD's median variability from 4.96% to 2.83% for stride time and from 8.19% to 5.80% for swing time. Set against gaitpdb's 2.41% and 3.67% (its cleaning already drops the stride either side of each turn, because its turns are estimated rather than observed), bout ends appear to account for most of the excess in stride time variability but only about half of that in swing time variability. The trimmed series are too short to settle this: a median of 9 strides on the weaker foot, about half the number one study found necessary for a reliable estimate [S21]. Variability cannot be the whole story either, because the models without the variability features transferred no better (0.521 and 0.576), and whether the short passes also distort the other features cannot be told from these data.

*Number of strides.* WearGait-PD's participants contributed fewer clean strides (median 32 on the weaker foot, against 81). Recomputing gaitpdb's test features from WearGait-PD's stride counts cost at most 0.008 AUC within gaitpdb, so stride count alone does not explain the failure.

*Age and the make-up of the groups.* WearGait-PD's controls were older than its patients (median 75 against 68 years), the reverse of gaitpdb (64 against 68), and people with essential tremor were not excluded from them [S9]. In the combined analysis, adding age and sex raised the ranking of WearGait-PD's participants (to 0.737 and 0.711) and lowered gaitpdb's (to 0.631 and 0.706): given age, a model partly learns how each cohort was recruited. Exploratory checks suggest that the gait models themselves learned some of the gait of ageing in gaitpdb, where patients are older than controls: within WearGait-PD's controls, their predicted probability of PD rose with age (Spearman ρ 0.641 and 0.556; 0.210 and 0.240 among its patients). Matching patients and controls on sex and age raised the cross-cohort AUC to 0.646 (0.539 to 0.750) and 0.670 (0.566 to 0.771), but these intervals overlap the main ones, 49 of the 56 pairs join participants from different sites, and at VA Puget Sound, where the groups' ages were similar, the AUC did not rise; how much age explains remains open. Disease stage overlaps without being the same: gaitpdb's patients were all at Hoehn and Yahr stage 2 to 3 (Table 1), whereas the spread reported for WearGait-PD's (2.15 ± 0.61 [S9]) is wider than patients confined to that range could produce. Medication state could not be compared: WearGait-PD did not control it and recorded only the time since the last dose [S9], and gaitpdb's demographic table has no medication field [S7].

*Hardware and event detection.* gaitpdb used eight force sensors under each foot [S7], WearGait-PD pressure insoles [S9], and one contact rule served both, chosen on agreement with WearGait-PD's walkway. At that threshold the insoles matched 83% of the walkway's footfalls one to one, with 95% limits of agreement of −0.09 to +0.09 s for stride time but −0.15 to +0.11 s for stance time and −0.10 to +0.15 s for swing time, so the phase-based features, swing fraction and swing-time asymmetry among them, carry more measurement error than stride time does. At 20 N, gaitpdb's own separation was numerically higher (0.831 against 0.789; not tested), but the cross-cohort result was the same (0.534 and 0.540): the threshold does not explain the failure, though the hardware may contribute.

*Recruitment site.* Two of WearGait-PD's three sites contributed only one group each. A model fitted on gaitpdb cannot have learned these sites, and at VA Puget Sound, the one site where neither site nor age is confounded with diagnosis, the AUC was 0.555 (0.398 to 0.701) and 0.554: no sign of a site effect, but too few participants (57) to rule one out. Measurement quality did differ by site: the insoles matched the walkway one to one for 80% of footfalls at the patient-only site against 84% to 88% at the others.

Standardising each dataset's features, which removes differences in their level and spread, left the AUCs at 0.535 and 0.538. A pure shift in level lowers the specificity of a carried-over threshold, as happened here (0.12), without lowering the AUC, which depends only on how participants are ranked; the low AUC means that the features rank WearGait-PD's participants poorly. An exploratory check agrees: within WearGait-PD each feature alone gave an AUC between 0.520 and 0.584, a logistic regression trained and tested there reached 0.595 (0.500 to 0.690), and swing fraction, lower in PD in gaitpdb (AUC 0.283), was not lower in PD across WearGait-PD (0.527).

## D. What the study shows and what it does not

The study shows that models fitted on five foot-force timing features in gaitpdb did not transfer to WearGait-PD (AUC 0.532 and 0.573), in an analysis whose code and inputs were committed before any model was scored against WearGait-PD's diagnoses. The result holds at either contact threshold and is not explained by stride count, by shifts in the features' level and spread, or by the variability features. The inputs were chosen with WearGait-PD's unlabelled feature distributions in view (stride-time asymmetry was dropped because it was eleven times higher there and did not separate the groups in gaitpdb), which if anything should have favoured transfer. The study suggests, but cannot establish, which differences matter most: the short passes inflate the variability features, yet models without them failed too; the scores' dependence on age points to ageing, yet the age-matched evidence is mixed. Protocol, hardware, population and site all change together between the two datasets, and two datasets cannot separate them. Nor can the study show that foot-force timing cannot detect PD elsewhere: the upper limits of the cross-cohort intervals reach 0.621 and 0.659, so a modest transfer is not excluded.

## E. What this means for patients and clinicians

Even within gaitpdb, the logistic regression found about half of the patients (sensitivity 0.53) at 90% specificity. PD affects at least 1% of people over 60 [S15]; at that prevalence, this operating point gives a positive predictive value of 0.057, so about 94 of every 100 people flagged would not have PD. That is a best case: the patients were already diagnosed, at Hoehn and Yahr stage 2 to 3, and the threshold was read off the same predictions it was evaluated on; for early, undiagnosed disease, which a screening tool would be for, the sensitivity is unknown. Carried to WearGait-PD, gaitpdb's threshold flagged most participants, patients and controls alike (sensitivity 0.93, specificity 0.12, positive predictive value 0.011). Because the cross-cohort AUC summarises every possible threshold, recalibrating the threshold locally could not rescue the model, and the exploratory within-WearGait-PD result (0.595) suggests that refitting it there would not be enough either. A classifier of this kind cannot be moved to another clinic or walking protocol without being validated there, and each false positive costs the person flagged worry, further tests and a referral.

## F. Limitations

Six further limitations bound these conclusions. First, the samples are modest (155 and 169 participants) and the intervals correspondingly wide; with about fifteen cross-cohort comparisons per model, any single one, such as the age-matched rise, should be read with that number in mind. Second, both cohorts compare diagnosed patients, mostly at stage 2 to 3, with volunteer controls, not the early, undiagnosed disease and other gait disorders of a screening population, and the labels are clinical diagnoses, which in a meta-analysis agreed with pathology in 80.6% of cases [S1]. Third, gaitpdb's turns were located from walking-path timing rather than observed, and its Si usual walk was assumed to be overground (Methods), while WearGait-PD's turns were labelled from video, so its cleaning kept the stride next to each turn. Fourth, WearGait-PD's three tasks were pooled, which adds about 10% to stride time variability, whereas each gaitpdb participant contributed one walk. Fifth, 11 of WearGait-PD's 181 participants aged 50 or over, 8 of them controls, had no insole data, and the contact threshold was chosen against its walkway, itself a measurement rather than ground truth. Sixth, gaitpdb records no ethnicity or socioeconomic information [S7], and WearGait-PD records race but nothing socioeconomic [S9], so how well either cohort represents a population that would be screened can be judged only in part.

## G. Next step

The most useful next step is to separate the walking protocol from the population. WearGait-PD's FreeWalk task, recorded with the same insoles on the same participants, leaves the session room for the hallways [S9]; comparing the features' separation in its straight corridor segments (stairs and chair cut out) with that in the short passes would show how much the passes remove, with population, hardware and site held fixed. Its sheets also hold the controls' motor examination scores, which would test directly whether controls with mild movement abnormalities look PD-like to the models. And because its protocol is rich in turns, one of the challenging conditions under which quantitative gait measures detect PD more sensitively [S3], turn-based measures could be taken from the same insole signals and evaluated within WearGait-PD under the same participant-grouped cross-validation.
