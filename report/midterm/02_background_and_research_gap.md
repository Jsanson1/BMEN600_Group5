# Section 2. Background and Research Gap

Because the diagnosis of PD rests on clinical examination, its accuracy depends on who makes it: across the 11 autopsy-confirmed studies in a 2016 meta-analysis, clinical diagnoses were correct ~81% of the time overall and ~74% of the time when made by non-specialists, with no improvement over 25 years and the poorest accuracy in early disease [S1]. Gait is an appealing basis for an objective aid because i) gait impairment is among the most common and disabling symptoms of PD, and ii) it is nonetheless rarely measured in routine care, even though quantifying speed, stride-to-stride variability and left-right asymmetry, particularly under challenging conditions such as dual tasking or turning, makes gait assessment more sensitive [S3].

The gait changes themselves are well established. Studies from the Tel Aviv group using force-sensitive insoles found that people with PD walk with shorter strides and higher stride-to-stride variability than healthy controls [S6], and that this variability i) increases under a secondary task [S4], ii) is independent of walking speed in the case of swing time [S5], and iii) is modulated by rhythmic auditory cueing [S6]. The recordings from these three studies were released as the PhysioNet “Gait in Parkinson’s Disease” database (eight force sensors per foot at 100 Hz during ~2 minutes of self-paced walking; 93 patients and 73 controls) [S7], [S8], which has become a common benchmark for gait-based PD classification [S11], [S13], [S14]. A larger multimodal dataset, WearGait-PD (100 patients and 85 controls; inertial sensors, pressure insoles and an instrumented walkway), was published in 2026 by a different group with different hardware [S9].

Machine learning has been applied to these data extensively: a 2024 systematic review found gait to be the most common input and SVMs the most common algorithm, with random forests also performing well [S10], and a recent PhysioNet study reports 98% accuracy for detection and Hoehn and Yahr staging [S11]. Interestingly, however, two issues recur. The first is evaluation: each participant contributed up to seven recordings, and when recordings rather than participants are split between training and test folds, the same individual appears in both. Saeb et al. showed that this “record-wise” cross-validation “often massively overestimates” accuracy and that almost half of the wearable-sensor studies they reviewed had used it [S12]; recent PhysioNet work still describes k-fold cross-validation over recordings without stating that each participant’s recordings were kept together [S11], the kind of leakage that [S13] identifies as the source of overestimated performance in earlier studies. The second is what happens when participants are kept separate: deep models on windowed force signals reached a mean AUC of up to 0.94 under repeated subject-wise splits [S13], and a 2026 participant-level pipeline reached 0.99 only with UPDRS and Hoehn and Yahr scores among its inputs; with gait features alone its held-out AUC fell to 0.84, and leave-one-study-out evaluation across the database’s three protocols gave anywhere from 0.50 to 1.00 [S14]. Both studies point to variability features as informative, however neither tested its model on an independent cohort [S13], [S14].

In light of these findings, the open question is not whether gait differs in PD, but whether a small set of interpretable spatiotemporal features computed from foot-force signals alone (i.e., without clinical scores) can separate patients from controls when tested the way a screening tool would be used: on people, recording protocols and cohorts that the model has never seen. To this end, this project will i) derive stride, swing and stance timing, their variability and their left-right asymmetry from the foot-force signals of both datasets, ii) compare logistic regression, an SVM and a random forest under repeated cross-validation on the two datasets combined, with each participant’s recordings kept in one fold and confidence intervals reported, and iii) test generalisation directly, by training on one dataset and testing on the other and, within PhysioNet, by holding out each of its three protocols in turn. Overall, the project asks whether the results reported for this benchmark hold under the validation that clinical use would require.
