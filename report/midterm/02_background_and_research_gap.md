Clinical diagnosis of Parkinson's disease (PD) is wrong in roughly one case in five. In a meta-analysis of studies with autopsy confirmation, the pooled accuracy of the clinical diagnosis was 80.6%, and 73.8% when the diagnosis was made mainly by non-specialists; accuracy had not improved over the preceding 25 years and was lowest in early disease [S1]. The number of people living with PD is projected to reach 25.2 million by 2050, a 112% increase from 2021 [S2], so most patients will continue to be diagnosed and followed outside specialist clinics. Gait is a natural target for an objective test. Gait impairment is among the most common and disabling features of PD, yet in routine practice it is described in words rather than measured, and measuring several features at once (speed, stride-to-stride variability, left-right asymmetry) is more sensitive to the disease than any single one [S3].

The gait changes in PD have been quantified in detail with force-sensitive insoles. Compared with age-matched controls, people with PD walk with shorter strides and higher stride-to-stride variability of stride time and swing time [S6]. The variability increases further when attention is divided by a second task [S4]; it is not explained by slower walking, since swing time variability is independent of speed [S5]; and rhythmic auditory cueing reduces it [S6]. The recordings from these three studies form the PhysioNet "Gait in Parkinson's Disease" database: vertical ground reaction force (VGRF) from eight sensors under each foot, sampled at 100 Hz during about two minutes of self-paced level walking, for 93 people with PD and 73 controls, with Hoehn and Yahr stage and UPDRS scores for the patients [S7], [S8]. It is the most widely used public benchmark for gait-based PD classification. A second, larger dataset became available in 2026: WearGait-PD, with inertial sensors, pressure insoles and an instrumented walkway for 100 people with PD and 85 controls, recorded with different hardware by a different group [S9].

Machine learning has been applied to these data many times. In a 2024 systematic review of wearable-sensor studies, gait was the most common input and support vector machines the most common classifier, with random forests and nearest-neighbour methods also performing well [S10]. On the PhysioNet VGRF records, recent papers report accuracies above 95%; one gradient-boosting model reached 98% for detection and Hoehn and Yahr staging [S11]. Two problems recur in this literature. The first is how the models were tested. Each participant contributed up to seven recordings, and when recordings rather than participants are split between training and test folds, the same person appears in both. Record-wise cross-validation "often massively overestimates" accuracy and was used by almost half of the wearable-sensor studies reviewed by Saeb et al. [S12]. Recent PhysioNet studies still report stratified k-fold cross-validation over recordings without stating that a participant's recordings were kept in one fold [S11], [S13], [S14]. The second problem is what happens when participants are kept separate. Deep sequence models trained on windowed VGRF with repeated subject-wise splits reached a mean AUC of 0.94 [S13]. A participant-level pipeline reported an AUC of 0.99, but only while UPDRS and Hoehn and Yahr scores were included as input features; with gait features alone the held-out AUC was 0.84, and leave-one-study-out evaluation across the three recording protocols gave AUCs between 0.50 and 1.00 [S14]. Both studies point to stride-timing variability as the most informative feature family, and the second adds left-right asymmetry [S13], [S14]. Neither reports a test on an independently collected cohort, and the second names external validation as the next requirement [S14].

The open question is therefore narrower than whether gait differs in PD. It is whether a small set of spatiotemporal gait features, computed from foot-force signals without any clinical score, can separate PD from controls at a useful level when the model is tested on people and recording conditions it has not seen, which is how a screening tool would be used. This project addresses that question. We will derive stride, swing and stance timing, their variability and their left-right asymmetry from the PhysioNet records; compare logistic regression, a support vector machine and a random forest under repeated cross-validation with each participant's recordings kept in one fold, reporting confidence intervals; and use leave-one-study-out validation across the Ga, Ju and Si protocols as the comparison against within-protocol performance. If time allows, the WearGait-PD insole data will serve as an independent test set. The project reuses an open benchmark and published feature definitions, and it asks whether the results reported for that benchmark hold under the validation that clinical use would require.
