# Section 2. Background and Research Gap

_Draft 3, 2026-10-05, rewritten after reading Jamie's own writing and cut back (about 4,300 characters, roughly 1.2 pages at 11 pt; trim further only if the assembled document runs over 6 pages). The live copy is the shared Google Doc; this file is the snapshot kept with the code. Citations are the S-numbers from `sources.md`. Prevalence and the affected population are left to Section 1._

PD is currently diagnosed on clinical grounds alone (i.e., from the history and a neurological examination), and that diagnosis is less accurate than one might expect: across the 11 autopsy-confirmed studies in a 2016 meta-analysis, clinical diagnoses of PD were correct ~81% of the time overall and ~74% of the time when made by non-specialists, with no improvement over 25 years and the poorest accuracy in early disease [S1]. Gait is an appealing basis for an objective aid to diagnosis because i) gait impairment is among the most common and disabling symptoms of PD, and ii) it is nonetheless rarely quantified in routine care, even though measuring several features together (e.g., speed, stride-to-stride variability, and left-right asymmetry) is considerably more sensitive to the disease than any single one [S3].

The gait changes themselves are well established. Studies from the Tel Aviv group using force-sensitive insoles found that PD patients walk with shorter strides and markedly higher stride-to-stride variability than age-matched controls [S6], and that this variability i) increases under a secondary task [S4], ii) is independent of walking speed in the case of swing time (i.e., not a by-product of walking slowly) [S5], and iii) decreases with rhythmic auditory cueing [S6]. The recordings from these three studies were released as the PhysioNet "Gait in Parkinson's Disease" database (eight force sensors per foot at 100 Hz during ~2 minutes of self-paced walking; 93 PD patients and 73 controls), now the standard public benchmark for gait-based PD classification [S7], [S8]. That said, a larger multimodal dataset, WearGait-PD (100 PD patients and 85 controls; inertial sensors, pressure insoles, and an instrumented walkway), was published in 2026 by a different group with different hardware [S9].

Machine learning has been applied to these data extensively: a 2024 systematic review found gait to be the most common input and SVMs the most common algorithm, with random forests also performing well [S10], and recent PhysioNet studies routinely report accuracies above 95% [S11]. Interestingly however, two issues recur. The first is evaluation. Each participant contributed up to seven recordings, and when recordings rather than participants are split between training and test folds, the same individual appears in both; Saeb et al. showed that this "record-wise" cross-validation "often massively overestimates" accuracy and that almost half of the wearable-sensor studies they reviewed had used it [S12], and recent PhysioNet studies still describe stratified k-fold cross-validation over recordings without stating that each participant's recordings were kept together [S11], [S13], [S14]. The second is what happens when participants are properly kept separate: deep models on windowed VGRF signals reached a mean AUC of 0.94 under repeated subject-wise splits [S13], and a 2026 participant-level pipeline reached an AUC of 0.99 only with UPDRS and Hoehn and Yahr scores among its inputs; with gait features alone its held-out AUC fell to 0.84, and leave-one-study-out evaluation across the database's three protocols gave AUCs anywhere from 0.50 to 1.00 [S14]. Both studies point to stride-timing variability (and, in the latter, asymmetry) as the informative features, however neither tested its model on an independent cohort [S13], [S14].

In light of these findings, the open question is not whether gait differs in PD, but rather whether a small set of interpretable spatiotemporal features computed from foot-force signals alone (i.e., without clinical scores) can separate PD patients from controls when tested the way a screening tool would be used: on people and recording conditions the model has never seen. To this end, this project will i) derive stride, swing, and stance timing, their variability, and their asymmetry from the PhysioNet records, ii) compare logistic regression, an SVM, and a random forest under repeated cross-validation with each participant's recordings kept in one fold, reporting confidence intervals, and iii) use leave-one-study-out validation across the Ga, Ju, and Si protocols as the primary comparison, with WearGait-PD as an independent test set if time permits. Overall, the project asks whether the results reported for this benchmark hold under the validation that clinical use would require.
