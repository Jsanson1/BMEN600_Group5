# Section 2. Background and Research Gap

_Draft 1, 2026-10-05, Jamie (lead) with Anna reviewing. About 560 words of body text. Citations are the S-numbers from `sources.md`; they become IEEE numbers when the sections are merged. Open points for the group are in the comment block at the end._

Parkinson's disease (PD) is diagnosed clinically, from the history and the neurological examination, and that diagnosis is less reliable than is often assumed. A meta-analysis of studies with pathological confirmation found a pooled accuracy of 80.6%, falling to 73.8% when the diagnosis was made mainly by non-specialists, with no improvement over the preceding 25 years and the greatest difficulty in early disease [S1]. The number of people affected is projected to reach 25.2 million by 2050, a 112% increase on 2021 that is driven mostly by population ageing [S2], so the shortage of movement-disorder specialists will widen rather than close. Gait impairment is one of the most common and disabling features of the disease, yet in routine care it is described rather than measured; quantifying several gait features at once, in particular speed, stride-to-stride variability and left-right asymmetry, is more sensitive to the disease than any single measure [S3].

The gait changes themselves are well characterised. Instrumented studies from the Tel Aviv group, using force-sensitive insoles, showed that people with PD walk with shorter strides and higher stride-to-stride variability than age-matched controls [S6], that this variability rises further when attention is divided by a secondary task [S4], that swing time variability is independent of walking speed and therefore not a by-product of walking slowly [S5], and that rhythmic auditory cueing reduces it [S6]. The recordings behind those three studies were released as the PhysioNet "Gait in Parkinson's Disease" database: vertical ground reaction force (VGRF) from eight sensors under each foot, sampled at 100 Hz during roughly two minutes of self-paced level walking, for 93 people with PD and 73 controls, with Hoehn and Yahr stage and UPDRS scores for the patients [S7], [S8]. It has become the standard public benchmark for gait-based PD classification. A larger multimodal dataset, WearGait-PD, with inertial sensors, pressure insoles and an instrumented walkway for 100 people with PD and 85 controls, was published in 2026 and offers, for the first time, an independent cohort recorded with different hardware [S9].

Machine learning on these data has a long record of strong numbers. A 2024 systematic review of wearable-sensor studies found gait to be the most common input and support vector machines the most common algorithm, with random forests and nearest-neighbour methods also performing well [S10]. On the PhysioNet VGRF records, recent papers report accuracies above 95%, for example 98% for a gradient-boosting model that also stages disease severity [S11]. Three limitations recur. First, the unit of evaluation. Each participant contributes up to seven recordings, and when recordings rather than participants are distributed across cross-validation folds the same person appears in both training and test data. Saeb and colleagues showed that this record-wise design "often massively overestimates" accuracy and that almost half of the wearable-sensor studies they reviewed used it [S12]; recent PhysioNet studies still describe stratified k-fold validation over recordings without stating that a participant's recordings were kept together [S11], [S13], [S14]. Second, performance is more modest once participant-level separation is enforced. Deep sequence models trained on windowed VGRF under repeated subject-wise splits reached a mean AUC of 0.94 [S13], and a participant-level pipeline reported an AUC of 0.99 only while UPDRS and Hoehn and Yahr scores were among its input features; with gait features alone the held-out AUC was 0.84, and leave-one-study-out evaluation across the three recording protocols produced AUCs anywhere between 0.50 and 1.00 [S14]. Third, the better-performing models are black boxes. The interpretable analyses that exist point to timing variability and asymmetry as the informative features, but they rest either on a single hold-out estimate without uncertainty [S14] or on post hoc explanations of a deep model [S13], and the most recent of them names external validation on an independent cohort as the outstanding requirement [S14].

What remains unresolved, then, is not whether gait differs in PD but whether a small, transparent set of spatiotemporal features derived from foot-force signals alone, without clinical scores, separates PD from controls at a useful level when tested the way a screening tool would be used: on people and recording conditions the model has never seen. This project addresses that part of the gap. It derives stride, swing and stance timing, their variability and their asymmetry from the PhysioNet records; compares logistic regression, a support vector machine and a random forest under repeated participant-grouped cross-validation with confidence intervals; and treats leave-one-study-out performance across the Ga, Ju and Si protocols as the primary comparison against within-protocol performance. If the WearGait-PD insole data can be processed in time, transfer to that cohort will be the external test. The question is deliberately narrow: it reuses an open benchmark and published feature definitions, and it asks whether the results reported for that benchmark survive the validation a clinical use would require.
