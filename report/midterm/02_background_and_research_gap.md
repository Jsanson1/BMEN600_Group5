PD is diagnosed primarily on clinical grounds [S15], and when checked against pathology, that diagnosis was correct ~81% of the time across the 11 studies of a 2016 meta-analysis and ~74% of the time when made by non-experts, with no improvement over 25 years and the poorest accuracy in early disease [S1]. Gait is an appealing basis for an objective aid because i) gait impairment is among the most common and disabling symptoms of PD, and ii) it is nonetheless rarely measured in routine care, even though quantifying speed, variability and asymmetry under challenging conditions such as dual tasking or turning makes the assessment more sensitive [S3].

The gait changes themselves are well established. In the three studies whose recordings make up the PhysioNet “Gait in Parkinson’s Disease” database, people with PD walked with shorter strides and higher stride-to-stride variability than controls [S6], and this variability i) increased under a secondary task [S4], ii) was independent of walking speed in the case of swing time [S5], and iii) was modulated by rhythmic auditory cueing [S6]. The database holds the vertical force under eight sensors per foot, sampled at 100 Hz during self-paced walking, for 93 patients and 73 controls [S7], [S8], and recent gait-based PD classifiers have used it [S11], [S13], [S14]. A larger multimodal dataset, WearGait-PD (100 patients and 85 controls; inertial sensors, pressure insoles and an instrumented walkway), was published in 2026 by a different group with different hardware [S9].

Machine learning has been applied extensively to gait data from wearable sensors: a 2024 systematic review found gait to be the most common input and SVMs the most common algorithm, with random forests also reporting promising accuracy [S10], and a recent study on the PhysioNet data reports 98% accuracy for detection and Hoehn and Yahr staging [S11]. Interestingly, however, two issues recur. The first is evaluation: participants in this database contributed one to seven recordings each, so when recordings rather than participants are split between training and test folds, the same individual can appear in both. Saeb et al. showed that this “record-wise” cross-validation “often massively overestimates” accuracy and that almost half of the wearable-sensor studies they reviewed had used it [S12]; recent PhysioNet work still describes k-fold cross-validation over recordings without stating that each participant’s recordings were kept together [S11], the kind of leakage that [S13] identifies as a cause of overestimated performance in earlier studies. The second is what happens when participants are kept separate: deep models on windowed force signals reached a mean AUC of up to 0.94 under repeated subject-wise splits [S13], and a 2026 participant-level pipeline reached 0.99 when UPDRS and Hoehn and Yahr scores were among its inputs, 0.84 on held-out participants with gait features alone, and between 0.50 and 1.00 when each of the database’s three protocols was held out in turn [S14]. Both studies point to variability features as informative; however, both evaluated their models only within this database, and the second calls for external validation [S13], [S14].

In light of these findings, the open question is not whether gait differs in PD, but whether a small set of interpretable timing features computed from foot-force signals alone (i.e., without clinical scores) can separate patients from controls when tested the way a screening tool would be used: on people, recording protocols and cohorts that the model has never seen. To this end, this project will i) derive stride, swing and stance timing, their variability and their left-right asymmetry from the foot-force signals of both datasets, ii) compare logistic regression, an SVM and a random forest under repeated cross-validation on the two datasets combined, with each participant’s recordings kept in one fold and confidence intervals reported, and iii) test generalisation directly, by training on one dataset and testing on the other and, within PhysioNet, by holding out each of its three protocols in turn. Overall, the project asks whether the results reported for this database hold under the validation that clinical use would require.
