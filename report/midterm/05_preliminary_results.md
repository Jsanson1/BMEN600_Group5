# Section 5. Preliminary Results

_Draft 4, 2026-10-06 (draft 2 with the cross-validation interval corrected for overlapping training sets, the comparison with single features added, the regression's penalty tuned by log-loss instead of AUC, and the odds ratios taken from the six-feature model the sentence describes), on the full database (306 recordings, 165 participants with recordings; all 311 files match PhysioNet's checksum list). The live copy is the shared Google Doc; this file is the snapshot kept with the code. Table 1 is `results/table1_participants.md` (from `scripts/make_table1.py`); Figure 1 is `figures/figure1_events_and_variability.png` (from `scripts/make_figure1.py`, which also writes `results/features_usual_walk.csv` and `results/features_usual_walk_by_group.csv`); the robustness numbers are in `results/event_detection_checks.txt` (from `scripts/check_event_detection.py`); the baseline model numbers are in `results/logreg_summary.txt` (from `scripts/run_logreg.py`); the comparison with single features is in `results/model_comparison.txt` (from `scripts/run_baselines.py` and `scripts/compare_models.py`, on the branch `jamie/T-022-model-comparison`)._

We downloaded version 1.0.0 of the database and wrote a first version of the loader, gait-event detection and feature code that the three models will share (`src/gaitpdb`; the scripts named above produce every number, table and figure in this section). The uncertainty named in our proposal, whether the gait phases can be identified from the force data alone, is resolved: a foot is on the ground while its total vertical force exceeds 20 N, heel strike and toe off are the crossings, and strides at turns, stops or with a dragged foot are removed by duration rules relative to each record's median stride.

Table 1 shows what the dataset contains, and three points in it shape the plan: the controls are younger than the patients (63.7 vs 66.3 years; 57.9 vs 61.6 in the Si study), although the PhysioNet description gives 66.3 years for both, so age has to be adjusted for in every model comparison; the patients are at Hoehn and Yahr stage 2 to 3, so whatever accuracy we reach applies to established disease and not to the early cases in which clinical diagnosis is weakest [S1]; and participants contribute one to seven recordings, which is why folds are split by participant rather than by recording.

Figure 1A shows the event detection doing what it should, with the markers on the rise and fall of the force and the turn at the end of the window excluded, and the pipeline reproduces the published dual-task effect: in the 21 Ga patients recorded both ways, stride-time variability and swing-time asymmetry rise under the counting task (p = 0.006 for each, `results/dual_task_check.txt`), as the source study reported [S4]. On the usual walk, mean stride time and cadence do not differ between the groups (p = 0.20 and 0.11), so the patients' slower walking speed in the demographics file (1.03 vs 1.24 m/s) must come mostly from shorter strides, which insoles cannot measure. Of the two features the literature singles out (Fig. 1B, 1C), stride-time variability is higher in PD but overlaps heavily (median CV 4.5% vs 4.1%; AUC 0.60), whereas swing-time asymmetry separates the groups considerably better (5.3% vs 1.7%; AUC 0.76, p < 10^-7), does not correlate with age in either group and holds within the 60 to 80 year band (AUC 0.75), while the variability difference shrinks there (AUC 0.58).

The research question stands; two things changed in the method. The variability feature proved fragile: a single double-length stride could double a record's CV, and before the relative stride rule the group difference was not significant (p = 0.18) and swung with the contact threshold (AUC 0.51 at 10 N against 0.65 at 50 N); with the rule it barely moves (0.59 to 0.61), and threshold sensitivity is now a standing check in Section 4. Asymmetry and swing-phase features move up the list of inputs.

As a first baseline under the planned evaluation, a logistic regression on six timing features (`scripts/run_logreg.py`) reaches an AUC of 0.81 (95% CI 0.75 to 0.88, corrected for the overlap between cross-validation training sets [S17]) and 0.76 (0.68 to 0.83) when each sub-study is held out in turn, a drop of 0.057 (0.025 to 0.093). Swing-time asymmetry (odds ratio 2.27 per SD of log(1 + x), the transform its linearity check called for), the swing fraction (0.43) and stride-time variability (1.46) carry the weight, and swing-time asymmetry alone, its direction learned from the training participants, gives 0.76 within and 0.75 across protocols; the other five features thus add 0.057 within a protocol (95% CI 0.002 to 0.113, an interval that only just excludes zero) and almost nothing across protocols (0.005, −0.049 to 0.065). Age and sex alone give 0.58 and add nothing to the gait features (0.80 within, 0.76 across). The drop across protocols is the quantity this project is about; the published participant-level results (0.84 with gait features only, 0.99 with clinical scores [S14]) are above both of our estimates.

The data can support a properly validated estimate of how separable established PD and healthy controls are from timing features alone, within one database and across its three protocols; they cannot support a diagnostic claim, since there are no early-stage or undiagnosed participants, no other movement disorders, and one laboratory's hardware. The largest remaining uncertainty is the heterogeneity between the sub-studies, which the leave-one-study-out comparison is designed to expose. Before the final submission we need to extract the same features from the dual-task and cued walks, run all three classifiers under the shared evaluation, and obtain access to WearGait-PD (a Synapse account and its data-use terms) for the external test, the one step we do not control.
